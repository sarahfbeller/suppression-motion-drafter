## ARGUMENT

### I. The Stop Was Unlawfully Prolonged Beyond Its Original Purpose

A traffic stop becomes unlawful if it is prolonged beyond the time reasonably required to complete the mission of the stop. *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348 (2015). Authority for the seizure ends when tasks tied to the traffic infraction are, or reasonably should have been, completed, and an officer may not extend an otherwise-completed stop, even briefly, to conduct a dog sniff or other unrelated investigation absent independent reasonable suspicion. *Id.* Where an officer develops reasonable, articulable suspicion of additional criminal activity during the stop's original mission, a brief extension to investigate that suspicion may be justified; the burden is on the government to establish that suspicion arose before, not after, the original mission concluded. *Id.*

A dog sniff is permissible only if it does not add time to the stop. *Illinois v. Caballes*, 543 U.S. 405 (2005).

### II. The Frisk of [Client] Was Not Supported by Reasonable Suspicion That He Was Armed and Dangerous

An officer may frisk a person only where there is reasonable, articulable suspicion that the particular person is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968); *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1979). The suspicion must be directed at the person frisked; suspicion of others nearby, or of the place, does not justify a frisk. *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1979). An officer's unparticularized suspicion or hunch is not enough. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968).

The same standard applies to a passenger during a traffic stop: the officer must reasonably suspect that the passenger is armed and dangerous. *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323 (2009). A passenger frisk during a traffic stop requires more than the passenger's mere presence in the vehicle. *Id.* General officer-safety concerns tied to the stop itself, without something specific to the passenger being frisked, do not satisfy this standard. *Id.*

### III. The Warrant Affidavit Contained Material Falsehoods or Omissions

A defendant is entitled to an evidentiary hearing on the validity of a search warrant affidavit upon a substantial preliminary showing that the affiant knowingly and intentionally, or with reckless disregard for the truth, included a false statement in the affidavit that was necessary to the finding of probable cause. *Franks v. Delaware*, 438 U.S. 154 (1978). If, after excising the false material or including the omitted information, the affidavit's remaining content is insufficient to establish probable cause, the warrant must be voided and the fruits of the search excluded. *Id.*
