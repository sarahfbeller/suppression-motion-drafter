Stop legality

No reasonable suspicion for the initial stop
Stop based on anonymous tip lacking indicia of reliability
Stop based on profile/pattern rather than particularized suspicion
Prolonged beyond time needed for the stop's original purpose (Rodriguez v. United States)
Checkpoint/roadblock without a valid primary purpose (Indianapolis v. Edmond)
Officer's mistake of law was unreasonable (contrast with Heien v. North Carolina, where a reasonable mistake can still support a stop)

Frisk scope

No reasonable suspicion the person was armed and dangerous
Pat-down exceeded a search for weapons (manipulation of soft objects, beyond "plain feel" — Minnesota v. Dickerson)
Frisk extended to a passenger without individualized suspicion
Frisk continued after any weapon concern was dispelled
Frisk expanded into the surrounding area/vehicle without separate justification

Consent validity

Consent not voluntary (coercive presence, number of officers, show of force)
Consent came after an illegal stop/detention (taint — did it purge or not?)
Search exceeded the scope of what was consented to
Third-party consent issues — actual vs. apparent authority (Illinois v. Rodriguez)
Consent withdrawn mid-search but search continued
"Knock and talk" consent obtained coercively

Warrant defects

No probable cause in the affidavit
Stale information in the affidavit
Lacks particularity — overbroad/general warrant
Franks violation — false statements or material omissions in the affidavit
Execution exceeded the warrant's scope (wrong areas/items)
Improper no-knock or nighttime execution
Anticipatory warrant's triggering condition wasn't met
Good-faith exception (United States v. Leon) — arguing it shouldn't apply

Plain view

Officer wasn't lawfully present when the observation was made
Incriminating character wasn't immediately apparent
Officer moved an object to see it (not truly "plain view")
Used to justify a broader search than the single item seized

Inventory search

No standardized policy governing the inventory
Impoundment itself was discretionary/unjustified
Search functioned as pretext for investigation, not routine inventory
Scope exceeded policy (e.g., opening locked containers without authorization)

Canine sniff

Sniff prolonged the stop beyond what the original stop required (Rodriguez)
Dog's training/reliability record not established
Sniff occurred at the curtilage of a home (Florida v. Jardines — trespassory sniff)

Miranda / custodial interrogation

Custody itself is disputed (would a reasonable person have felt free to leave?)
Statement was spontaneous, not the product of interrogation
Warnings were incomplete or inadequate
Waiver wasn't knowing, voluntary, and intelligent
Invocation of silence or counsel wasn't honored
"Question-first, warn-later" technique (Missouri v. Seibert)
Public-safety exception misapplied
Confession involuntary under due process, independent of Miranda (coercion, promises, deception)
